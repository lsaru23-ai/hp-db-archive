The Ancestors of Seok-am Lee Dong-beom

The Lee Dong-beom Family of Hampyeong: A 128-Year Bond | Special Edition 1

[EN] English edition · Translated from the Korean original · 한국어 원문

The ten main parts told a story that began with Seok-am Lee Dong-beom and moved down through the generations: the "Jinsa Lee" who welcomed Baekbeom Kim Koo (also romanized as Kim Gu), Chun-gang Lee Jae-seung who carried on the head family, and the stories of my grandfather and grandmother.

In this special edition, I would like to change direction and go upward. In what records do the elders before Lee Dong-beom survive? And what can and cannot be said from those records?

1. Names Linked in the Genealogy

On page 1080 of volume 11 (the Hyou-gong branch) of the Hampyeong Lee Clan Comprehensive Genealogy (Hampyeong Issi Daedongbo), our family's direct line is connected in a single line: from Lee Bung-seo (李鵬緖) through Lee Don-hwa (李敦華), Lee Gyeong-heon (李警憲), Lee Gye-song (李啓松), Lee Dong-beom (李東範), and Lee Jae-seung (李載昇) to my grandfather, Lee Sang-rae (李祥來).

Counting from me, Lee Bung-seo is my eighth-generation ancestor and the first generation of our head family. Lee Don-hwa is my seventh-generation ancestor, Lee Gyeong-heon my sixth, and Lee Gye-song my fifth. Seok-am Lee Dong-beom is my great-great-grandfather.

Lee Gye-song's courtesy name, Boksam (復三), and his other name, Hong-gyu (弘奎), as well as the official titles of Lee Don-hwa and Lee Gyeong-heon, are recorded on page 1080 of the genealogy. Lee Dong-beom's pen name Seok-am (石庵) and courtesy name Gyeong-yeon (敬然), and Lee Jae-seung's pen name Chun-gang (春崗), follow the existing sources introduced in the main parts and are being compared with the original genealogy page.

2. The First Generation of the Head Family: Lee Bung-seo

The genealogy entry for Lee Bung-seo records that his courtesy name was Ikji (翼之), and that he was born in the gihae year of King Jeongjo's reign (1779) and died in the gisa year of King Sunjo's reign (1809).

The entry for Lee Don-hwa, who succeeded him, records a separate biological father and shows that he continued Lee Bung-seo's line. The genealogy also records that the tomb inscription for Lee Bung-seo was composed by his son, Lee Don-hwa.

3. Seventh-Generation Ancestor Lee Don-hwa — Manho of Dagyeong Garrison

The courtesy name of Lee Don-hwa (1797–1860) was Jajeong (子貞). His genealogy entry records an official post in 1856: "manho of Dagyeong Garrison" (多慶鎭萬戶). Manho (萬戶) was one of the military posts of the Joseon dynasty.

The official titles recorded in a genealogy, however, must be distinguished from actual appointment and service. When Lee Don-hwa was actually appointed and how long he served have not yet been compared with court records such as the Seungjeongwon Ilgi (Diaries of the Royal Secretariat) or the Ilseongnok (Records of Daily Reflection). The genealogy also records that the tomb inscription for Lee Don-hwa was composed by his grandson, Lee Gye-song, and that a stele pavilion was erected by his great-grandson, Lee Dong-beom, then a county magistrate.

Meanwhile, old documents related to Lee Don-hwa are currently kept at the head family house in Hampyeong. Their contents and their relation to the official title recorded in the genealogy will be introduced when the findings are ready for publication.

4. Sixth-Generation Ancestor Lee Gyeong-heon — Jobong Daebu and Gyogwan

The courtesy name of Lee Gyeong-heon (1825–1855) was Inbeom (仁凡). His entry records Jobong Daebu (朝奉大夫) and gyogwan (敎官, instructor). Jobong Daebu was a junior fourth-rank civil service grade.

This too is a record written in the genealogy. Whether it was a rank actually conferred or one granted posthumously, and in which county he served as an instructor, require further verification.

The Seungjeongwon Ilgi, checked since, records that in 1891 Lee Gyeong-heon was posthumously appointed dongmong gyogwan (instructor of youth) for his filial piety (Record 024). Whether the genealogy's "gyogwan" refers only to this posthumous title remains to be checked.

5. Two Ancestors Laid to Rest in Unnam-myeon, Muan

The Hampyeong Lee Clan Comprehensive Genealogy records the graves of Lee Don-hwa and Lee Gyeong-heon as being in Muan. The two graves are still in present-day Unnam-myeon, Muan County, South Jeolla Province, and our family has confirmed them in person.

Because the genealogy uses old place names, establishing the exact correspondence between those names and present administrative districts is a separate task. The inscriptions on each grave have also not yet been read and compared. In this piece, I record only the existence of the graves and the genealogy entries, and do not disclose the exact location of the graves.

6. Fifth-Generation Ancestor Lee Gye-song — The Name Hong-gyu and a Jeolchung Janggun Gravestone

Seok-am Lee Dong-beom was the son of Lee Gye-song (李啓松, 1841–1884). The Hampyeong Lee Clan Comprehensive Genealogy records Lee Gye-song's other name as Hong-gyu (弘奎), and also preserves a record related to an official post, "mucheomchu" (武僉樞), in 1871.

The gravestone of "Lord Lee Hong-gyu" (李公弘奎) in the family burial ground is inscribed with Jeolchung Janggun (折衝將軍), a senior third-rank military title of the upper grade, and Cheomji Jungchubusa (僉知中樞府事). Thanks to the other name "Hong-gyu" in the genealogy, we were able to connect this gravestone to Lee Gye-song.

The rank and title on the front of the gravestone point in the same direction as the genealogy's "mucheomchu" record. The circumstances of his appointment and whether he actually served, however, require further confirmation. The inscription on the back of the gravestone has also not yet been fully read.

7. Official Careers Leading to Lee Dong-beom

Seok-am Lee Dong-beom's own official career is recorded in contemporary sources. As introduced in Part 5, the Maeil Sinbo profile of July 8, 1917, states that he rose to the senior third rank of tongjeong daebu and served as a councilor of the Jungchuwon (Central Advisory Council). Because this is a career summary reported by a newspaper, however, his actual appointment and service must be confirmed separately in official documents. Among these, his service as county magistrate of Mangyeong and Eunyul is confirmed in the Official Gazette (Gwanbo) of the Korean Empire: the record that in August 1903 he left the post of county magistrate of Mangyeong and was appointed county magistrate of Eunyul. The name "Lee Mangyeong" (李萬頃) that appears in the 1909 police record in Part 10 also appears to be a form of address related to his having served as county magistrate of Mangyeong.

Placing side by side the official titles of earlier generations left in the genealogy and on gravestones, and Lee Dong-beom's career as confirmed in contemporary records, one can see a family's history continuing across generations. The records of the earlier generations, however, rely mostly on the genealogy and gravestones, so the weight of the evidence is not the same as for Lee Dong-beom's records.

8. How Far Can "Wealth and Honor for Eight Generations" Be Said?

Baekbeom-ui Gil (The Path of Baekbeom), published in 2018, introduces Lee Dong-beom as a descendant of "a distinguished family that enjoyed wealth and honor for eight generations." The lineage of earlier generations confirmed this time through the Hampyeong Lee Clan Comprehensive Genealogy runs five generations, from our eighth-generation ancestor Lee Bung-seo, the first generation of the head family, to my great-great-grandfather; we have not yet obtained records showing the family's standing in generations before that.

There is one more point to make clear. Old records of official titles and later landholding or wealth are separate kinds of evidence, so an official career is not in itself the basis for the "3,000 seok." I introduce the official records of our elders only as clues for examining the family's history, and will not use them as materials proving the scale of its wealth.

9. Remaining Questions

Putting this together has also made clear what needs to be confirmed: the record of Lee Don-hwa's appointment as manho of Dagyeong Garrison and the contents of the old documents kept at the head family house; Lee Gyeong-heon's rank, and how the genealogy's gyogwan entry relates to the posthumous title of 1891; Lee Gye-song's mucheomchu record and a reading of the inscription on the back of his gravestone; and a comparison of the inscriptions on the two graves in Unnam-myeon, Muan, with the genealogy entries.

I will compare, one by one, the names left in the genealogy and on gravestones with the records of their own times, and continue to write down as much as can be confirmed.

In the next special edition, I will tell the story of the women of the Lee Dong-beom family: Lee Ok, Lee Hak, and my grandmother Shin Mun-ran.
