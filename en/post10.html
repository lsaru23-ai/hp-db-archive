Where Did the 3,000 Seok Go? — What the Records Say and Do Not Say

The Lee Dong-beom Family of Hampyeong: A 128-Year Bond | Part 10

[EN] English edition · Translated from the Korean original · 한국어 원문

In Part 9, I found the name of my great-grandfather, Chun-gang Lee Jae-seung, on an old original cadastral map. Following a name left on the land, the next question came naturally: where did all that land and wealth go?

This part is not a piece that gives answers, but one that sets out questions. By separating what the records tell us from what they do not yet tell us, I would like to trace carefully the path our family's wealth has taken.

1. The Phrase "3,000 Seok"

As introduced in Part 5, the 2003 survey report on the Lee Jae-hyeok House describes my great-great-grandfather Seok-am Lee Dong-beom as a man known to be wealthy with 3,000 seok (a measure of rice; shorthand for a large landed fortune). I also grew up hearing similar stories from family elders.

This figure, however, is an expression that survives in later records and tradition. The scale of 3,000 seok has not been confirmed in ledgers or documents of the time, so we take the phrase only as a clue that lets us guess at the family's circumstances in those days.

2. 1909: A Dispatch from the Righteous Army

There is a record showing that the Lee Dong-beom family of that time was singled out as a target of a demand for a large sum in military funds. It is a confidential report submitted on August 3, 1909, by the chief of Naju Police Station to the director of the Police Bureau of the Korean Empire's Ministry of Home Affairs. It is published in the Righteous Army volumes of Materials on the History of the Korean Independence Movement (한국독립운동사자료), compiled by the National Institute of Korean History.

According to the report, on July 30, 1909, a demand for military funds issued in the name of a Righteous Army (uibyeong) commander was delivered to "Lee Mangyeong" (李萬頃) in the town of Hampyeong. Because the report contains both 李萬頃 and 李東範, it can be seen as a record connected to Lee Dong-beom, who had served as county magistrate of Mangyeong. The accompanying dispatch reads: "咸平郡內 李萬頃宅 軍需金 一萬圓 從速來納于軍中事" — a demand that the house of Lee Mangyeong pay 10,000 won in military funds to the army without delay.

Ten thousand won was a very large sum at the time. The total capital of the (old) Bank of Korea, which began operating in November of the same year, was 10 million won; the 10,000 won demanded was one-thousandth of that. That the Lee Dong-beom family was singled out as a target for such a large demand is meaningful, but the amount demanded alone cannot establish the family's actual wealth or how the local community regarded the family at the time.

What this record proves, however, is only that a demand was made. Whether my great-great-grandfather actually paid, and if so how much, cannot be confirmed with the materials found so far. We therefore do not read this record as evidence that the family's wealth flowed to the Righteous Army, but leave it as a starting point for finding follow-up records.

Meanwhile, Baekbeom-ui Gil (The Path of Baekbeom), published in 2018, contains a passage in which Lee Hyeon-seok, a former director of the Hampyeong Cultural Center, is said to have described my great-great-grandfather as someone who, though he did not take part in the independence movement directly, had independence fighters coming and going and "contributed funds for independence, openly and in secret." The author was relaying an explanation found on the internet, and it is not known whether this refers to the same matter as the 1909 demand. We have not yet found any record that connects the "demand" left in the police report with the "testimony" handed down in later times.

3. Names Left on the Land

On the Gigak-ri original map introduced in Part 9, the name of my great-grandfather Lee Jae-seung was written. A single sheet of the original map cannot tell us the overall scale of the family's land or how its ownership changed, but it does confirm that Lee Jae-seung's name remains on Original Map No. 9 of Gigak-ri.

According to family tradition, the family owned land not only in Hampyeong but also in Muan. This story, too, is family tradition not yet confirmed by primary sources, and is a subject for future research.

4. Grain Requisitions, Land Reform, and War

In Part 7, drawing on Lee Hak's memoir, I told the story of rice hidden in a barn to escape the forced grain requisitions of the early 1940s, and of my grandfather being taken to the police station. Lee Hak's memoir conveys that the Lee Dong-beom family suffered under the pressure of the requisitions at the time.

After liberation came even greater change. With the enactment of the Farmland Reform Act in 1949 and its implementation from the following year, a ceiling was placed on the farmland a landlord could hold, and the government purchased the remaining land and distributed it to farmers. Landlords whose farmland was purchased were issued land value certificates stating the amount of compensation, which was to be paid in installments over several years.

Then the Korean War broke out almost immediately. Amid the turmoil and soaring prices of wartime, the value of the land value certificates fell sharply, and history records that not a few landlord families lost their standing in this period.

Baekbeom-ui Gil (2018) also introduces my great-great-grandfather as "a wealthy man with 3,000 seok," writing that his line as a great landowner was cut off through the land reform after liberation, the Yeosu–Suncheon incident, and the Korean War. This account alone, however, does not prove when or why the family's wealth actually declined. The same book's phrase, "a distinguished family that enjoyed wealth and honor for eight generations," has also not yet been confirmed by other records, and we leave it as a point requiring further verification.

Our family, too, guesses that the period when our wealth greatly declined was around the time of the land reform and the war. This, however, is the family's guess; when, how much, and in what way our family's land actually passed to others has not yet been confirmed in records.

5. What We Do Not Yet Know

Putting this together has made clear the records we still need to find: police records after the 1909 demand for military funds, changes in landholding through inheritance and sale from the 1910s to the 1930s, records of distribution and compensation at the time of the land reform, and primary sources on the land in Muan.

In asking where the wealth went, too, I intend to follow only as far as the records confirm.

In the next piece, I will pause for breath, look back on the ten parts of this series, and share our plans going forward.
