Seok-am Lee Dong-beom — The Life of "Jinsa Lee"

The Lee Dong-beom Family of Hampyeong: A 128-Year Bond | Part 5

[EN] English edition · Translated from the Korean original · 한국어 원문

In the previous four parts, I followed the bond between Kim Gu and our family in chronological order: the two weeks of 1898, the 1946 reunion and the Gwangmyeong (光明) calligraphy, and the head family house and the Lee Jae-hyeok House that still stand today. From this part on, I would like to introduce the people in that story one by one. The first is the "Jinsa Lee" of Baekbeom Ilji, my great-great-grandfather, Seok-am Lee Dong-beom.

1. The First of Three Generations

Seok-am Lee Dong-beom (石庵 李東範, 1869–1940) stands first among the three generations of grandfathers in our family. His pen name is handed down as Seok-am (石庵), and his courtesy name as Gyeong-yeon (敬然). The line of the head family, continuing through his son Lee Jae-seung and grandson Lee Sang-rae, begins with him. Until now he has been called only the "Jinsa Lee" in the anecdote about Kim Gu; this time I would like to follow his life as a person in his own right.

2. The Master of the House in 1898

In 1898, when Kim Gu came to the house of "Jinsa Lee" in Hampyeong, Lee Dong-beom was about thirty years old — a young master leading the household. Baekbeom Ilji records the names Lee Jae-hyeok and Lee Jae-seung as young brothers who were learning to read and write in that house. At the time, the eldest son, Lee Jae-seung, was about eight, and the second son, Lee Jae-hyeok, about five. The hospitality that kept a one-night guest for two weeks was introduced in Part 2. The story of how those two brothers, then small children, went to see Kim Gu in Seoul half a century later is in Part 3.

3. The Lost Seal

Among the newspaper records found so far, the earliest place where my great-great-grandfather's name appears is, unexpectedly, the advertisement column. On August 23, 1899, the year after Kim Gu's visit, a short notice appeared at the bottom of page 3 of the Hwangseong Sinmun.

It reads as follows: Lee Dong-beom, former chambong, lost his name seal, carved in both relief and intaglio, on the night of the 2nd day of the 7th lunar month, and from that day the seal is no longer to be used. At the end is even a neatly added signature, 李東範 白 — "announced by Lee Dong-beom."

I laughed for a long time at the fact that, 127 years ago, my great-great-grandfather lost his seal and even placed a newspaper notice about it. As someone who tends to leave things behind wherever I go, I found this passage oddly reassuring. Perhaps carelessness runs in the family, too.

But it also contains information too valuable to laugh off: as early as 1899 he was already called a "former chambong" (前參奉), a low-ranking official post. The 2003 survey report on the Lee Jae-hyeok House dates his service as chambong to 1902, but this notice shows that the post came earlier.

4. Official Career and Return Home

The profile in the Maeil Sinbo of July 8, 1917, describes his official career as follows: he first entered office as chambong of Jogyeongmyo (肇慶廟), was promoted in the 6th year of Gwangmu (1902) to the senior third rank of tongjeong daebu, served as a councilor of the Jungchuwon (Central Advisory Council), and became county magistrate of Mangyeong in North Jeolla Province.

The Official Gazette (Gwanbo) of the Korean Empire also preserves records from this period. In August 1903 he left the post of county magistrate of Mangyeong and was immediately appointed county magistrate of Eunyul in Hwanghae Province; by July of the following year, the Gazette already lists him as "former county magistrate of Eunyul." The phrase "after leaving office" in Part 1 refers to this time.

The article states that he gave up office and returned to his hometown of Hampyeong in order to care for his parents. It introduces him, then forty-nine, as a man of strong public spirit and charity, and singles out his devoted care for his seventy-year-old mother as the household's most notable trait.

5. Work in His Hometown

His life after returning home is recorded in later records and in family tradition. The 2003 survey report states that he took the lead in building the Sesimjeong (洗心亭) pavilion in Hampyeong Park and that he was known as a wealthy man with 3,000 seok (a measure of rice; shorthand for a large landed fortune).

The same report also tells of the family's pavilion, the hexagonal yukmojeong. When the pavilion that his father, Lee Gye-song, had built in the early 1880s was about to be taken over for a new road, he is said to have dismantled the pavilion in 1924, moved the stones from its pond, and built a new lotus pavilion (蓮亭). With the disappearance of the hexagonal pavilion, the spaces of Kim Gu's time were substantially changed once in the 1920s. This change is also an important clue for understanding the relationship between the two houses examined in Part 4.

6. Remaining Questions

Seok-am Lee Dong-beom passed away in 1940. He was no longer alive in 1946, when Kim Gu tried to find his old benefactor's house again, and so his two sons went to see Kim Gu in his place. Putting this together also made clear the questions still to be confirmed: a detailed reading of the 1917 article, the content of the Gazette records from his time as county magistrate of Mangyeong, records concerning the construction of the Sesimjeong, and the basis for the title "jinsa."

I believe that writing down separately what is confirmed by records and what is handed down in the family is the most honest way to leave behind my great-great-grandfather's life.

In the next part, I will introduce my great-grandfather Lee Jae-seung, the eldest son who inherited the head family house.
