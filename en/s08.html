Before we begin

The last detailed chronology is about my eighth-generation ancestor Lee Bung-seo (李鵬緖, 1779–1809). He is the earliest of the seven generations this series has traced, and the one for whom we have found the fewest records.

All that is confirmed about him is a few lines in the genealogy. This post sets out those lines and brings the series, which has moved back through seven generations, to a close.

2. What the records show

The genealogy records his dates and that his son Lee Don-hwa composed his tomb inscription. By the genealogy's dates his was a short life of a little over thirty years, and his son lost his father at a young age.

The inscription record shows how tomb inscriptions and pavilions were handed on in this family. The son Lee Don-hwa composed the inscription for his father Lee Bung-seo; Lee Don-hwa's own inscription was composed by his grandson Lee Gye-song, and its pavilion built by his great-grandson Lee Dong-beom. Across the generations, they recorded and tended one another's graves.

3. What the records do not show

Office and life

No office is recorded in the genealogy. That it is not recorded there does not mean he held none. We have not yet searched official records such as the Seungjeongwon Ilgi systematically for him; that is where the next research begins.

The inscription stone

We have not yet seen the inscription his son composed, or the stone itself.

4. Having come back through seven generations

This series began with the nearest generation, my grandfather Lee Sang-rae, and moved back through Lee Jae-seung, Lee Dong-beom, Lee Gye-song, Lee Gyeong-heon and Lee Don-hwa to Lee Bung-seo.

The further back we went, the fewer the records. For my grandfather there were a school diploma, family photographs and a memoir. For my great-grandfather and great-great-grandfather, newspapers and the Official Gazette were dense. Further back there remained a few lines of the Seungjeongwon Ilgi and a few family documents, and finally a single line in the genealogy.

Still, seven generations began from that one line, and the records each generation left shed light on one another. A son composed his father's inscription, a great-grandson built a pavilion, and the memory of filial piety reappeared in a newspaper generations later.

5. What comes next

The detailed chronologies end with this part. The questions left as "what the records do not show" in each part carry on as research: checking the military examination roster, the relationship between Lee Don-baek and Lee Don-hwa, the dates of Lee Jae-seung's service as township head, and how the 「光明」 calligraphy was received, among others. Newly confirmed records will be added to the Archive one at a time, and corrections will be kept as "Correction records."

Closing

We have followed seven generations on the principle of writing only as far as the records reach, without inventing a story where the records end. Thank you for reading.
