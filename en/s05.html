Before we begin

The fourth detailed chronology is about my fifth-generation ancestor Lee Gye-song (李啓松, 1841–1884). The genealogy notes that he was "also named Hong-gyu (弘奎)," so this post uses both names. He was the father of my great-great-grandfather Seok-am Lee Dong-beom, and the author of the tomb inscription of my seventh-generation ancestor Lee Don-hwa.

His records run along two lines: the military examination diploma (hongpae) kept by the family, and the name "Lee Hong-gyu" that appears several times in the Seungjeongwon Ilgi. This post sets the two side by side and separates what is confirmed as his from what is inferred.

2. What the records show

The firmest record is the diploma kept by the family. That he passed the military examination in the 4th month of 1871, second place in the eulgwa class, is confirmed by the matching characters on the front of the diploma and on the folded title strip. How we first read "third" and corrected it to "second" is described in "How We Correct the Record."

Only one entry in the Seungjeongwon Ilgi names the place: the 1877 record of "Lee Hong-gyu, former owijang of Hampyeong," contributing 1,500 nyang to famine relief. It shows that the Lee Hong-gyu of Hampyeong had already served as owijang by then.

3. What the records do not show

Are the owijang records really his?

The owijang records of 1871 to 1883 give no place. Because they begin in the same year as the diploma and the 1877 record says "former owijang of Hampyeong," we think they are likely his. We have not yet ruled out another man of the same name, and plan to check the residence, father and clan seat recorded in the list of successful candidates (bangmok).

Did the 1877 proposal lead to an appointment?

A proposal to give him "a suitable post" is not the same as an actual appointment. We have not found later appointment records.

The formal name of the examination and his overall rank

The diploma alone cannot establish which examination it was or his place among all successful candidates. This also needs the bangmok.

The gravestone titles and the "同中樞" of 1913

We have not established how the gravestone titles relate to the "同中樞" of 1913, or when the gravestone was erected.

Yungmojeong

When the pavilion was built and what it looked like rest on a later survey report. How it relates to the space where Kim Gu stayed in 1898 remains a research question, as discussed in Series Parts 4 and 5.

4. Two names, two lines of records

The genealogy calls him Gye-song; the diploma and the Seungjeongwon Ilgi call him Hong-gyu. Because the genealogy records both ("also named Hong-gyu"), the two can be read together. Compiling his chronology was a reminder that family records and state records can point to the same person under different names.

Closing

My fifth-generation ancestor's chronology consists of one firm pillar, the diploma, surrounded by several records of "the same name." The pillar is confirmed; tying all the surrounding records to him needs one more key, the bangmok.
