A Name on the Map — "李載昇" on the Original Cadastral Map of Gigak-ri

The Lee Dong-beom Family of Hampyeong: A 128-Year Bond | Part 9

[EN] English edition · Translated from the Korean original · 한국어 원문

Through Part 8, I introduced the members of our family one by one, from my great-great-grandfather Seok-am Lee Dong-beom to my grandmother Shin Mun-ran. In this part, I would like to turn my eyes away from people for a moment and look at the land and spaces where they lived.

The starting point is an old map: a sheet densely ruled with a grid, on which the boundaries and numbers of land parcels are marked, and here and there people's names are written in brush.

1. Hampyeong on Grid Paper

A map like this is called an original cadastral map (地籍原圖). It is the original map made during the land survey project of the Japanese colonial period, when land was surveyed village by village and the boundaries and numbers of each parcel were drawn in. The copy we have obtained is filed as a 1915 document according to its file record, but we are rechecking the actual year of preparation on the original.

Along the right edge of the map, a title is written vertically identifying what the map is: "咸平郡咸平面箕閣里原圖 二十二枚之內 第九號" — that is, No. 9 of the twenty-two sheets of the original map of Gigak-ri, Hampyeong-myeon, Hampyeong County.

2. The Three Characters 李載昇

Following the crowded parcels one square at a time, a familiar name catches the eye. Written vertically inside one parcel is 李載昇 — the name of my great-grandfather, Chun-gang Lee Jae-seung, introduced in Part 6.

It is hard to put into words how it felt to find my great-grandfather's name myself on a map more than 100 years old. It was different from seeing the name in newspaper articles or the genealogy — like finding his name on the map of Hampyeong in the very era he lived.

That a name is written on the original map, however, is a different matter from the whole ownership history of that land having been established. Ownership and changes in ownership must be confirmed in separate registers, such as the land survey register or the land ledger, and we have not yet checked which present-day land this map's parcel corresponds to.

3. The Document We Were Looking At

There is also something we corrected while preparing this part. We had first filed this material as a land ledger, but when we unfolded the original again, it turned out to be not a register but a map — an original cadastral map.

We also confirmed that two files we had saved under parcel-by-parcel names in fact contain the same Original Map No. 9. We therefore decided to withhold judgment on the names written in the file names that cannot be confirmed directly on the original, and to write in this piece only the names we have read and confirmed with our own eyes.

I learned once again that, in handling old documents, knowing what kind of document you are looking at matters as much as what is written on it.

4. The Landscape of Hampyeong 100 Years Ago

If the map shows the boundaries of land, photographs show what Hampyeong looked like at the time. Kim Hui-tae, who has long studied the local history of Hampyeong, has compared photographs of Hampyeong-eup in the 1917 Jeonnam Sajinji (全南寫眞誌, a photographic record of South Jeolla) with old maps, town plans, and aerial photographs to trace how the space of Hampyeong town has changed.

1917 is the very year the Maeil Sinbo, introduced in Part 5, presented my great-great-grandfather as a "notable of South Jeolla." Kim Hui-tae explains that Gigak-ri lies on the left of this panoramic photograph of Hampyeong-eup and Naegyo-ri on the right. The place name Gigak-ri written on the old map thus reappears in the old photograph as a village landscape. This does not mean, however, that it reveals the location of any specific parcel on the original map.

5. A Memory Reaching as Far as Muan

According to family tradition, the family owned land not only in Hampyeong but also in neighboring Muan. This memory has been handed down in the family, and we have not yet obtained primary sources to confirm the specific location and ownership of the land in question. At this stage, therefore, this story is left not as a confirmed fact but as a subject for future research.

6. Remaining Questions

Putting this together also made clear the questions still to be confirmed: the exact year the Gigak-ri original map was prepared, a reading on the original of the other family names written on the map, comparison with the land survey register, and primary sources on the land in Muan.

Even a single name on a map, I intend to write down only as far as I have confirmed it with my own eyes.

In the next part, I will continue with another family story found in the records.
